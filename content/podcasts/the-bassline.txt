@ DESMOND = Desmond Hale | 360
@ ROSA = Rosa Quintero | 450

# Introduction
DESMOND: This is Liner Notes. I'm Desmond Hale. Every record you love has a list of names printed in very small type somewhere on the sleeve, and on this show we go looking for the people behind those names. My guest today has played bass on, by her own estimate, somewhere north of four hundred recordings. Pop records, country records, film scores, a few jingles she would prefer we not mention.
ROSA: Please don't mention the jingles.
DESMOND: I won't mention the jingles. Rosa Quintero, welcome to Liner Notes.
ROSA: Thank you, Desmond. It's strange to be on this side of a microphone. Usually when I'm in a room like this, I'm the one in the corner trying not to make any noise between takes.

# Becoming a session player
DESMOND: Let's start at the beginning. How does someone become a session bassist? Because I don't think most people know that's even a job.
ROSA: Most people don't. And honestly, when I was young, I didn't either. I grew up in San Antonio, and my uncle played bass in a conjunto band, and I used to sit under the table at family parties and watch his right hand. Not his face, not the singer, his right hand. There was something about how little he moved. Everyone else was dancing and sweating and he was just standing there, completely calm, holding the whole thing together.
DESMOND: The quiet center of the room.
ROSA: Exactly. And I think I decided pretty early that I wanted to be that person. I didn't want to be the one out front. I wanted to be the one everybody was leaning on without realizing it.
DESMOND: And how did that turn into a career?
ROSA: Slowly, and then all at once. I played in bar bands for years. I taught lessons. I played in pit orchestras for musicals, which is the best training in the world, because you have to read anything they put in front of you and you can never, ever be late. And then a producer I'd done a few demos for called me on a Tuesday afternoon and said, our bass player is sick, can you be here in an hour. And I went. And that was the first real session.
DESMOND: What do you remember about that first day?
ROSA: Terror. Pure terror. And the coffee. The coffee was very good. Studios always have very good coffee, because they know everyone is terrified.

# The craft of serving the song
DESMOND: I want to talk about what the job actually is, because I think there's a misunderstanding that a session player is just someone who plays what's written.
ROSA: Sometimes it is. Sometimes there's a full chart and they want exactly that, and your job is to play it perfectly, with great feel, on the first or second take. But a lot of the time, especially in pop and rock, there's no chart. There's a demo. Maybe it's the songwriter singing over an acoustic guitar on their phone. And the producer says, give us something.
DESMOND: Give us something.
ROSA: Give us something. Which is the most terrifying and the most wonderful sentence in the business. Because now you're not just playing. You're writing. You're deciding what this song sounds like underneath. And the trick, the thing that took me years to understand, is that the best part is almost never the most impressive one.
DESMOND: Say more about that.
ROSA: Young players, and I was one, want to show you what they can do. They want to fill every space. But the song doesn't need you to be impressive. The song needs you to make the singer sound like the most important person in the world. So most of what I do, honestly, is decide what not to play.

# The take that wasn't supposed to be kept
DESMOND: Which brings us to the story I asked you here to tell. There's one bassline in particular that has followed you around for a long time.
ROSA: It has. It really has. And the funny thing is, it was never supposed to exist.
DESMOND: Tell me about the day.
ROSA: So this was a long time ago. I was booked for a three-hour session on a single for a singer who was, at that point, not well known. Nobody in the room thought this song was the big one. It was a filler date. The producer was tired, the drummer had a flight to catch, and we had been working on this one song for most of the afternoon, and it just wasn't sitting right. The verses felt stiff. Everyone was playing correctly, and it was completely dead.
DESMOND: That happens.
ROSA: It happens all the time. So the producer calls a break, and everyone goes to get coffee, and I stayed in the room because I was frustrated and I wanted to figure it out. And I started just playing around. Not the part. Just noodling. I took the root notes I'd been playing, which were very straight, very on the beat, and I started leaving out the first note of every bar.
DESMOND: Leaving it out.
ROSA: Leaving it out. Just letting the drum take the downbeat alone, and then coming in a little late, a little behind, almost like the bass was answering the kick drum instead of agreeing with it. And then I added this little climb at the end of every second bar, three notes, nothing fancy. And I was just playing it for myself. I didn't think anyone was listening.
DESMOND: But somebody was.
ROSA: The engineer. The engineer had never stopped recording. This was something engineers do, by the way, the good ones. They just leave the tape running because they know people do their best playing when they think it doesn't count. So the producer comes back with his coffee, and the engineer says, you need to hear this. And he plays back me noodling, completely alone, no drums, no singer, just this lopsided little bass figure.
DESMOND: And what did the producer say?
ROSA: He didn't say anything for a while. He just listened to it twice. And then he said, that's the song. Build everything around that. And they literally did. The drummer missed his flight. We stayed another three hours. They rearranged the whole track so that everything was leaning on this part I'd invented by accident while everyone else was getting coffee.
ROSA: And I remember the singer coming in that evening, after we'd rebuilt the track. She hadn't been there for any of it. She put on the headphones, and they played her the new version, and she just started laughing. Not because it was funny. Because suddenly she knew how to sing it. The verse that had been stiff all day, she sang in one take. She said, oh, it's a sway. I thought it was a march, but it's a sway.
DESMOND: The bass told her how to sing it.
ROSA: The bass told her how to sing it. That's the thing people don't understand about the low end. You don't really hear it consciously, most of the time. But your body hears it. It tells you whether to stand still or lean, whether to rush or relax. Change the bass, and you change what the singer's body thinks the song is about.
DESMOND: And the record?
ROSA: The record did very well. Much better than anyone expected. And for years afterward, I would be in a grocery store, or in a taxi, or at a wedding, and I would hear that bassline. Just that little lopsided thing, coming out of a speaker. People would be humming it in line at the bank. And nobody had any idea that it was me. My name was on the sleeve, in that very small type you mentioned, but nobody reads that.
ROSA: The first time I heard it on the radio, I was in a cab in the rain, and the driver had it on, and I made him turn it up. And he looked at me in the mirror like I was crazy, because I was sitting in the back seat with my eyes closed, playing air bass on my knee.
DESMOND: Did you tell him?
ROSA: I did. I said, that's me, on the bass. And he said, sure it is. And turned it back down.
DESMOND: How did that feel? Hearing it everywhere and being invisible?
ROSA: I've thought about this a lot. And I think at the time, honestly, it felt wonderful. It felt like I had a secret. I'd be at a party and someone would put the song on and everyone would start dancing, and I'd think, you're dancing to something I did with my hands in an empty room. It felt like magic.
DESMOND: And now?
ROSA: Now it's more complicated. Because over the years I watched the business change, and the small type got smaller, and then on a lot of streaming services, it disappeared entirely. You can listen to a song a thousand times and have no way of knowing who played on it. And that's not just about credit for me. It's that the listener loses something too. If you love that bassline, you might love the other four hundred things I've played on. But there's no road from the song back to me.
DESMOND: No road back to the person.
ROSA: No road back to the person. And I think that's what we've lost. Not the money, although, yes, also the money. It's the connection. The ability for someone who loves a sound to go and find where it came from.

# Credit and connection
DESMOND: I want to stay on that, because it's something we talk about a lot on this show. When you were growing up, how did you find the players you loved?
ROSA: Liner notes. Exactly like the name of your show. I would buy a record, and I would read every word on the back. Who played bass, who engineered it, which studio. And then I'd go to the record store and look for other albums with the same names on them. That's how I found half the musicians I've ever loved. It was like following a trail of breadcrumbs.
DESMOND: And that trail is harder to follow now.
ROSA: It's harder. It's not impossible. There are people working on it, there are databases, some services are getting better about showing credits. But it's not the default. The default is a song title and an artist name, and everyone else is invisible.
DESMOND: What would you want listeners to do differently?
ROSA: Be curious. That's all. When something moves you, ask who made it. Not just the singer. Ask who played the drums. Ask who wrote the string arrangement. Ask who engineered it. Because those people are out there, and they're making other things, and you might love those things too.

# Leaving space
DESMOND: You said earlier that most of your job is deciding what not to play. I want to come back to that, because I think it's true of a lot more than bass.
ROSA: I think it's true of almost everything. When I teach young players now, the first exercise I give them is to play a song with half the notes. Take whatever part they've worked out, and cut it in half. And they hate it. They feel naked. And then they listen back, and it sounds twice as good.
DESMOND: Why does that work?
ROSA: Because silence is an instrument. When you leave a space, the listener leans in. They fill it with their own anticipation. If you play every beat, nobody's leaning in. They're just being told.
DESMOND: There's something about trust in that.
ROSA: Yes. You're trusting the listener to meet you halfway. And honestly, I think a lot of music now is afraid of that. Every second is full, because everyone's worried that if there's a gap, someone will skip. So you end up with records that never breathe.
DESMOND: Do you think listeners actually want the space?
ROSA: I think listeners want to feel something. And it's very hard to feel something when you're never given room to. The records I love most, the ones I've played a thousand times, all have air in them. You can hear the room. You can hear somebody decide not to play.
DESMOND: You can hear somebody decide not to play. That might be my favorite sentence anyone has said on this show.
ROSA: You can have it. No charge.

# Closing
DESMOND: Rosa, before we go, what are you working on now?
ROSA: I'm actually making my own record, for the first time, at the age of fifty-two. All bass, all instrumental, recorded in my living room. I don't know if anyone will listen to it. But I wanted to know what it would sound like if I played everything I've spent thirty years leaving out.
DESMOND: I can't wait to hear it. Rosa Quintero, thank you for coming to Liner Notes.
ROSA: Thank you, Desmond. And thank you to every engineer who ever forgot to stop recording.
DESMOND: This has been Liner Notes. I'm Desmond Hale. Read the small type.
